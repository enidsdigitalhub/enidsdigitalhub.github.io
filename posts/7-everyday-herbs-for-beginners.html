7 everyday herbs for beginners: what they're loved for and what science says

Herbs belong in kitchens, teacups and family memories, not in miracle-cure claims. If you're curious about herbal teas and cooking with herbs but tired of the hype, this is a calm place to start. Here are seven everyday herbs, each with what it's loved for, what research can (and can't) tell us so far, and when to tread gently.

This post is for education only and is not medical advice. Please talk to a doctor or pharmacist before using herbs as a remedy, and especially if you are pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medication or managing a health condition.

1. Ginger

Loved forWarming teas, stir-fries and that familiar settle-your-stomach feeling.

What science saysGinger is one of the better-studied kitchen herbs. Some studies suggest it may help with mild nausea, but results vary and it isn't a substitute for medical care.

Tread gentlyLarge amounts can cause heartburn. Check with a professional if you take blood-thinning medicine.

2. Chamomile

Loved forA gentle, honey-like evening tea that's part of many bedtime routines.

What science saysStudies on chamomile and sleep or relaxation are small and mixed. Much of its comfort may come from the ritual of a warm cup itself.

Tread gentlyAvoid it if you're allergic to plants in the daisy family, such as ragweed.

3. Peppermint

Loved forA fresh after-dinner tea and a cooling flavour.

What science saysPeppermint oil capsules have been studied for some tummy complaints, but a cup of peppermint tea is much milder and less studied.

Tread gentlyPeppermint can make heartburn or reflux worse for some people.

4. Cinnamon

Loved forPorridge, baking and spiced warm drinks.

What science saysYou'll see big claims online, but research results are mixed and inconsistent. Enjoy it as a spice, not a treatment.

Tread gentlyCommon cassia cinnamon contains coumarin, which can be harmful to the liver in large amounts. Normal food amounts are fine; high-dose supplements are a different story.

5. Lemon balm

Loved forA soft, lemony tea from the mint family that's easy to grow at home.

What science saysA handful of small studies have looked at lemon balm for mood and sleep. It's promising but early.

Tread gentlyAsk a professional first if you take thyroid medication or sedatives.

6. Hibiscus

Loved forA tart, ruby-red tea that's lovely iced on a warm day.

What science saysSome studies have found modest effects on blood pressure, but it's not a replacement for prescribed treatment.

Tread gentlyCheck with your doctor if you take blood-pressure medicine, and avoid it in pregnancy unless advised otherwise.

7. Rosemary

Loved forRoast potatoes, bread and that wonderful garden scent.

What science saysEarly research has looked at rosemary's aroma and focus, but it's far too soon for firm conclusions.

Tread gentlyCooking amounts are fine. Concentrated rosemary oil should never be swallowed.

A simple way to start

Choose one herb you already like, enjoy it as a food or tea for a couple of weeks, and notice how you feel. Keep notes. Go slowly, and always ask a professional before using herbs alongside medicine.
